By selecting crops that maintain living roots year-round, fix nitrogen, support pollinators, and diversify plant communities, regenerative agriculture strengthens soil health, increases resilience to climate stress, and promotes long-term sustainability. Some examples are:

Nitrogen-Fixing Legumes Legumes such as clover, vetch, peas, and beans are particularly valuable because they fix atmospheric nitrogen, naturally enriching the soil and reducing the need for synthetic fertilizers.

Deep-Rooted Plants Plants like tillage radish and rye have deep root systems that break up compacted soil, improve aeration, and enhance water infiltration. These roots also help recycle nutrients from deeper soil layers to the surface.

Flowering and Pollinator-Friendly Crops Species such as phacelia and buckwheat provide nectar and pollen for pollinators, supporting biodiversity and ecosystem health. These crops also contribute organic matter to the soil when incorporated as green manure

Crop Rotation and Diversity Regenerative agriculture emphasizes rotating crops and increasing plant diversity. Alternating between cereals, legumes, and other species improves soil structure, reduces pest and disease cycles, and enhances microbial activity. For example, a rotation might include corn, soybeans, oats, and cover crops to maintain nutrient balance and soil resilience.

Additional Practices Some regenerative systems integrate agroforestry or intercropping, where trees or shrubs are grown alongside crops to provide shade, reduce erosion, and enhance habitat diversity. Edge-of-field plantings and vegetated buffers can also support soil and water conservation.
